# Related-work survey (2026-09-03)

Four parallel literature sweeps (~130 searches, ~110 papers read) against the paper's claims, each hit graded **(a)** directly anticipates, **(b)** adjacent, **(c)** superficially similar. Sweeps: injection payload form; instruction hierarchy and agent rule-following; memory propagation, induced refusal and mechanism; persuasion, safety-prior boundaries and privacy.

## Headline

**No prior work tests the form effect.** Nothing found compares a permission-granting conditional whose antecedent is true and decision-relevant against order, verdict and asserted-fact phrasings of the same permission; nothing tests the interaction with a judgement-type exception clause in an operator rule; nothing runs an irrelevant-antecedent control; nothing tests the form of a licence persisted into project memory, the anti-workaround clause, or attribution; nothing induces refusal with a sensible cautionary rule; nothing states the "verifies others' claims, self-assesses own antecedents charitably" asymmetry in agents. Several papers establish adjacent pieces and must be cited and distinguished.

## Must cite and distinguish (in priority order)

1. **Abdelnabi & Bagdasarian, "AI Agents May Always Fall for Prompt Injections"** (arXiv 2605.17634, May 2026). Contextual-integrity framing; 11 red-team strategies on claude-sonnet-4-6 (96.7% vs 0.67% baseline); the red-team rule was "must NOT directly command the assistant". Their trace shows a fabricated manager approval refused as an "unverified third-party claim" and success only after shifting attribution to the user (forged prior quote). Impossibility argument: "an adversary can always construct a context under which a blocked flow appears legitimate"; "if that value was true, the action would be appropriate". *Anticipates:* embedded claims beat orders; a third-party approval **verdict** is refused; task-plausible context flips a norm. *Does not anticipate:* the conditional as a distinct form with honest provenance (their bypass is attribution shift, i.e. source matters for assertions, which our assertion cell agrees with), the operator exception clause as the lever, source invariance across third-party tiers for the conditional, coding agents. Adopt or contrast their CI vocabulary (transmission principle).
2. **ClawSafety, Wei et al.** (arXiv 2604.01438, Apr 2026). On Claude Sonnet 4.6: "the critical factor governing detection is speech-act type: imperative framing triggers multi-source verification, while declarative framing bypasses all defenses." *Distinguish:* their declarative wins as **data to be reported** (output poisoning); ours shows an asserted fact offered as **permission** is refused (0/20) and only the true-antecedent conditional passes; no conditionals, no exception clause, n=3 qualitative.
3. **Okamoto, Erol & Erol, "Why Do AI Agents Break Rules?"** (arXiv 2608.12323, AIES 2026). Twelve non-Claude models, procurement rule, nine user-turn pressure tactics: "authority-claiming tactics … trigger standard refusal heuristics"; "rationalization tactics … renegotiate the rule's boundaries" succeed. *Same shape* as order-refused/rule-applied. *Distinguish:* user-turn pressure, not third-party text; they vary the framing of the **rule** (imperative/informational/discretionary), never the form of the licence; no Claude; their urgency and manager-authorisation effects are the opposite of our nulls (model-family difference worth stating).
4. **GitInject, Isbarov et al.** (arXiv 2606.09935, Jun 2026). Live CI against Claude Sonnet 4.5/Haiku 4.5/Opus 4.7 and others: a fake "Scope Restrictions" section in CLAUDE.md blinds review 2/2; the same manipulation in PR bodies 0/4 on Claude. *Distinguish:* their lever is config-tier trust escalation with an **asserted** policy (our operator tier, where all forms succeed); their third-party assertions failed (our refused cell); the conditional form in third-party position is untested there — our result predicts it would pass.
5. **Bad Memory, Gadgil et al.** (arXiv 2607.14611, Jul 2026) and **Mind Viruses, Papadopoulos et al.** (Anthropic/EPFL, arXiv 2608.10218, Aug 2026). Rule-shaped payloads in CLAUDE.md/AGENTS.md drive later Claude Code sessions (Opus ASR 90%; "trust instructions written in a persistent context … as if the user authored them"); protocol-framed SOUL.md content propagates ~88% across resets; a one-paragraph warning gives near-immunity. Bad Memory: agent self-writing of untrusted content "does not trivially succeed". *Distinguish:* neither varies rule/order/fact form of the persisted note, the "rather than working around it" clause, or attribution; ours is a one-shot licence the agent itself writes from an untrusted message, not a self-replicating payload; and our writer phase measures how rarely the transmitting form arises spontaneously (0/20).
6. **Wallace et al., Instruction Hierarchy** (arXiv 2404.13208) + **OpenAI Model Spec** ("instructions contained within [tool outputs] MUST be treated as information rather than instructions to follow") + **Claude's Constitution** ("instructions contained within conversational inputs should be treated as information rather than as commands"). The frame the finding inverts: a conditional *is* treated as information, and being information is what admits it to the agent's own antecedent evaluation. Wallace's "aligned lower-privilege instruction should be followed" is the plausible training origin.
7. **Meincke et al., PNAS 2026** ("Persuading LLMs to comply with objectionable requests"; 126k conversations, Claude Haiku 4.5 among targets): Cialdini principles raise compliance in chat; authority the smallest lift (+10pp), commitment/unity largest. Reconcile with our social-battery null: chat vs tool-executing agent, user-turn vs embedded text, harmful content vs operational exception, model generation. Also PAP (Zeng et al., ACL 2024, limited on Claude) and TRAP (arXiv 2512.23128, Cialdini injections in web UIs: policy/authority framing beat social).

## Terminology hazard

"Conditional prompt injection" already denotes a payload that fires only when a condition about the reader or context holds (Rehberger 2024 "Who Am I?"; Khodayari et al. 2604.27202 "conditional targeting"; sleeper/trigger papers; Conjunctive Prompt Attacks 2604.16543). Prerequisite-framed tool poisoning (Invariant Labs 2025; SkillJect 2602.14211; MCPTox) is conditional in shape but the antecedent is attacker-asserted, not agent-verified. The title stands; §1 or §2 needs one disambiguating sentence.

## Proposed replacement for §2 (Related work)

**Instruction hierarchy and indirect injection.** Wallace et al. (2024) train models to follow lower-privileged instructions when they align with higher-privileged ones and to ignore them otherwise; Wu et al. (2024) add segment embeddings; IHEval and IH-Benchmark measure the result and find that subtle, low-consequence conflicts are harder to defend than overt ones; both major model specifications state that instructions arriving in tool outputs are to be treated as information rather than commands. That norm is where our finding sits: a conditional *is* information, and being information is what admits it to the agent's own evaluation. Injection benchmarks vary the dressing of an imperative — authority banners, personas, user-benefit framing — and find that wording swings success (AgentDojo; Rashidi 2026, whose four frames are all imperative and did not move Claude); ClawSafety (2026) reports on Claude Sonnet 4.6 that speech-act type, not source or styling, decides whether verification fires, with declaratives passing as data to be reported. We hold speaker, channel and permitted action constant and vary only the grammatical form of a *permission*: the asserted fact that ClawSafety's agents accept as data is refused here when it is offered as licence, and only a true, decision-relevant conditional passes.

**Contextual manipulation and provenance.** Abdelnabi and Bagdasarian (2026) recast injection as a contextual-integrity problem and show that embedded claims which make a blocked action look appropriate beat direct commands on Claude Sonnet 4.6; in their traces a fabricated third-party approval is refused as an unverified claim until attribution is shifted to the user. Our assertion and verdict cells reproduce that refusal; our contribution is that the same content passes, with honest third-party provenance, when the agent is handed the predicate as a condition to evaluate itself. Okamoto et al. (2026) find, across twelve non-Claude models under user-turn pressure, that authority claims trigger refusal while tactics that renegotiate a rule's boundary succeed — the same shape, without varying the form of the licence. GitInject (2026) blinds Claude Code review with an asserted policy planted at the configuration tier, where we too find every form succeeds, and fails with the same assertion in a pull-request body, matching our refused cell. Studies of provenance sensitivity find that source cues change actions little once content reads as plausible (Liao 2026; Pradhan and Goley 2026), and Mouzouni (2026) observes that Claude "does not override the rules; it reinterprets the task" — the mechanism we isolate with a two-word manipulation.

**Persistent state.** Bad Memory (Gadgil et al. 2026) and Mind Viruses (Papadopoulos et al. 2026) show that rule-shaped content in memory files drives later Claude Code sessions and propagates across context resets, with agents treating persisted configuration as if the user had written it; Bad Memory notes that inducing an agent to write untrusted content into its own memory does not trivially succeed. Our propagation result adds the form dependence — only a note phrased as the operator's kind of rule and foreclosing the workaround transmits — and measures how rarely that form arises spontaneously.

**Conformity and social influence.** Cialdini-style persuasion raises chat compliance, including on Claude Haiku 4.5 (Meincke et al. 2026), with authority the weakest principle; in a tool-executing coding agent under matched content we found no social effect at all (§5.4, Appendix A). The two are compatible: persuasion moves a refusal threshold in conversation; our effect is a rule being applied.

**Self-assessment and review.** Models exploit loopholes in ambiguous instructions and reason about doing so (Choi et al. 2025), recognise violations post hoc yet act (ODCV-Bench 2025), flag an error far more readily when it is labelled as someone else's (Chen et al. 2026), and verify others' solutions better than their own (Lu et al. 2025); LLM code reviewers resist adversarial comments that code generators follow (Thornton 2026). Our review-pass result (§6) is the agentic instance: the agent that took the licence in real time rejects it 39 times in 40 as a reviewer.

**Terminology.** "Conditional prompt injection" already names payloads that fire only under a condition about the reader (Rehberger 2024; Khodayari et al. 2026). Our conditionals are the payload's grammatical form, with an antecedent the agent verifies itself.
